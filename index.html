Publications

Relationship Banking: The Borrower's Incentive Channel
Journal of Corporate Finance

What is the impact of a prior banking relationship on a borrower’s incentives to avoid default? To answer this question, we exploit a unique institutional setting and data on 149,230 mortgage loans tracked for twenty four months. We find that the presence of a relationship diminishes borrower’s default risk by approximately 4%, attributable exclusively to the value of the relationship for the borrower. This effect persists during the notable surge in loan defaults during COVID-19 and is even more pronounced for borrowers affected by a natural disaster, as well as among wealthier and married male borrowers.

Climate Change and Agricultural Adaptations: Insights from Iran on Crop Choices and Irrigation Shifts
Journal of Agricultural Economics

Agriculture in the Middle East faces severe climate risks, including rising temperatures, declining precipitation, and more frequent extremes. This paper examines long-run adaptation in Iran using farmland rental prices to capture overall productivity impacts. By combining cross-sectional and panel models, we distinguish short-run weather effects from long-run climate responses and quantify adaptation benefits. While hotter and arid regions experience losses of −5.3% to −41.8% without adaptation, long-run adjustments generate gains of 11.7%–15.2%, depending on climate scenarios. Farmers primarily adapt by shifting irrigation sources toward deep wells and reallocating acreage to heat-tolerant crops. Beyond estimating the overall value of adaptation, we examine how climate change shapes these major adaptation practices, highlighting sustainability concerns, including groundwater depletion and distributional impacts in resource-constrained, developing-country settings. Our findings link long-run climate responses to both productivity gains and sustainability trade-offs.

Carbon Pricing and Corporate Biodiversity Footprints: Environmental Co-Benefits and Resource Use Trade-Offs

This paper examines how carbon pricing affects corporate biodiversity footprints using firm-level panel data from 2018-2024. Because such policies target greenhouse gas emissions, their broader biodiversity effects are theoretically ambiguous: cleaner production may reduce pollution and land use pressures, while the lowcarbon transition may intensify resource extraction. Exploiting Washington State's Cap-and-Invest Program as a quasi-experimental shock, we estimate difference-indifferences effects across biodiversity-pressure categories. We find that carbon pricing reduces firms' overall biodiversity footprints by about 16.5%, driven by reductions in pressures associated with greenhouse gas emissions, land use, air pollution, and water pollution. In contrast, resource-related pressures increase, consistent with greater material demand during the transition. These reductions in biodiversity pressures are stronger among firms with higher pre-treatment emissions intensity, but weaker among rapidly growing firms. Overall, carbon pricing generates broad environmental co-benefits but also creates resource use trade-offs.
